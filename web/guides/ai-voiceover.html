How to make a free AI voiceover from text

Short answer: write the script, paste it into Text 2 Speech, choose a neural voice, add [break:…] markers where the narration should pause, and press Generate Audio. You get the voiceover as an MP3 or WAV file plus SRT and VTT subtitles timed to it, ready to place on a video timeline.

Prepare the script

  • One part per scene. Keep each part under 5,000 characters; a separate file per scene is easier to re-do and to place on a timeline.
  • End every sentence with punctuation. Full stops, question marks and exclamation marks give natural pauses, and each sentence becomes its own caption in the subtitle files.
  • Check the wording first. Preview Audio gives an instant rough read-back with your device's own voice, which is enough to catch awkward phrasing before you generate.

Pace it with pauses

A pause marker inserts an exact silence into the generated audio; it is never read aloud and never appears in the subtitles.

Type thisSilence added
[break:300ms]0.3 seconds
[break:500ms] or Ctrl+B0.5 seconds
[break:1.5s]1.5 seconds
[break:2s]2 seconds

The gap you hear is slightly longer than the marker, because the speech on either side keeps its own short natural edges: in a test, adding [break:2s] between two one-word sentences made the clip 2.9 seconds longer. A generation can hold 25 markers, each up to 30 seconds and 60 seconds in total.

Generate the voiceover

  1. Choose the Voice Language of the script and pick a narrator; listen to two or three with their Preview buttons.
  2. Paste the first scene into Input Text and add your pause markers.
  3. Choose MP3, or WAV if your video editor prefers it, and press Generate Audio.
  4. Listen to the result. To change a line, edit the text and generate again; each version stays in the results list.
  5. Download the audio and the ⬇ SRT file of the version you keep, and rename both with the scene number.
  6. Repeat for each scene with the same voice, speed and pitch.

Worked example: a three-scene explainer

This short explainer about watering houseplants was generated scene by scene with the voice Aria (US) at normal speed on 31 October 2026. The script, exactly as typed:

Scene 1: Most houseplants die from too much water, not too little.[break:1s] In the next minute, you will learn a simple test that tells you exactly when to water.

Scene 2: Push one finger into the soil, up to the second knuckle.[break:500ms] If the soil feels dry at your fingertip, water slowly until it runs out of the drainage holes.[break:500ms] If it still feels damp, wait two or three days and test again.

Scene 3: That is the whole method.[break:1s] Test first, water second, and check again next week.

Each scene was pasted on its own, without the Scene label. To see what the markers add, every scene was also generated once with the markers removed:

SceneCharactersMarkersAudio lengthWithout markersCaptions
1 · Hook1541 × 1 s10.8 s9.7 s3
2 · Method2402 × 500 ms15.9 s14.6 s6
3 · Close881 × 1 s7.6 s6.5 s2

The three files add up to 34.3 seconds. Each one-second marker lengthened its scene by about 1.1 seconds, and the two half-second markers together by 1.2 seconds, so plan for slightly more silence than you type. Scene 2 produced six captions from three sentences because long sentences are split after ten words. Saved as scene-01.mp3/scene-01.srt and so on, the files drop onto a timeline in order. To write a script for a target length, use the characters-per-second planner on the AI voice generator page.

Adjust speed and pitch

Speed changes how fast the voice talks, from −100% to +100%, without changing the words. In a test, +50% made a sentence play in two-thirds of the time (5.1 s to 3.5 s) and −50% made it twice as long (10.2 s). Pitch moves the voice up or down by up to 100 Hz. The slider moves in steps of 5; type any whole value into the number box next to it.

Fit a voiceover to a fixed length

Speed works in proportion: the new length is roughly the normal length divided by (1 + speed ÷ 100). Measured examples:

SpeedLength compared with normalMeasured
−50%× 2.0A sentence went from 5.1 s to 10.2 s
−20%× 1.25A passage went from 56.6 s to 70.8 s
+10%× 0.91Scene 2 above went from 15.9 s to 14.5 s
+50%× 0.67A sentence went from 5.1 s to 3.5 s

When a scene has to fill an exact slot, such as a 15-second clip, work in this order: generate at normal speed and read the length in the result; close a small gap with speed in steps of 5 or 10; close a large gap by cutting or adding a sentence, which sounds more natural than a big speed change; and fine-tune with pause markers, whose length is exact. Voices also differ in pace, by about a fifth between the fastest and slowest English voices in the voice guide's measurement, so choose the voice before you time anything.

Add it to a video

  1. Import the audio file into your video editor and place it on the timeline.
  2. Import the SRT file from the same generation as a caption track. Its times are measured from the start of that audio file, so align the two clips.
  3. For a video on your own web page, use the VTT file in a <track> element.

Troubleshooting

A pause marker was read aloud

Only markers in the form [break: number ms] or [break: number s] become silence; anything else is ordinary text and is spoken. Upper case and spaces inside the brackets are fine.

TypedResult
[break:750ms], [break:1.5s], [BREAK: 2 s]Silence
[pause:1s], (break:1s)Read as text: wrong word or brackets
[break:1sec], [break:1m]Read as text: only ms and s are units
[break:1,5s]Read as text: use a full stop for decimals

One scene sounds different from the others

Check that every scene used the same voice, speed and pitch: the settings stay as you left them, but a ticked or unticked Speed box is easy to miss. Each result shows its voice name, so compare the labels in the results list.

Captions appear before the voice starts

The subtitle times start at the beginning of the audio file. If you trimmed silence from the start of the clip in your editor, or moved the audio without the captions, shift the caption track by the same amount.

"Too many [break] markers" or "Total pause length exceeds 60 s"

A generation allows 25 markers and 60 seconds of pauses altogether. Split the scene in two, or leave longer silences to the video editor.

Limitations

  • One voice per generation: for several speakers, generate each speaker's lines separately and arrange them in the editor.
  • No speaking styles, emotions or SSML: pacing comes from punctuation, pause markers and speed.
  • No voice cloning: only the listed voices are available.
