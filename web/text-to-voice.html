Text to voice: turn writing into a natural voice

Short answer: text to voice means typing or pasting writing and getting it back as speech. In Text 2 Speech you set the language of your text, choose one of its voices (each tied to a regional accent), optionally adjust speed, pitch and pauses, and press Generate Audio. It is free, needs no account, and takes up to 5,000 characters per generation.

What decides how the voice sounds

Six settings shape the result. Only the first two need a decision; the rest default to normal speed, normal pitch and no extra pauses.

Turn text into a voice, step by step

  1. Open the converter and set Voice Language to the language your text is written in.
  2. Click a voice. To shorten the list, use Gender or type a country into Voice Search; press ▶ beside a voice to hear its sample.
  3. Type or paste the text, or import a .txt, .md or .text file.
  4. Tick Speed or Pitch to change them, and type an exact value into the number box if the slider's steps of 5 are too coarse.
  5. Add pauses where a reader would stop for breath or effect.
  6. Press Generate Audio, listen to the result, and download it as MP3 or WAV.

Language and accent

Text 2 Speech offers 322 voices in 75 languages and 142 regional variants (catalog of 31 October 2026). The variant is the accent: English alone comes in 14, from American and British to Indian, Nigerian and Kenyan English, and Spanish in 22. The country code after each voice name tells you which one you are hearing.

Match the language before anything else. A voice is made for its own language, and text in another language may come out mispronounced or produce no audio at all. Accents, multilingual voices and a quick way to compare candidates are covered in how to choose a voice and accent, and every voice is listed on the voices and languages page.

Picking one voice out of several

Voices in the same language differ more than you might expect, in sound and in pace: six English voices took between 5.6 and 6.8 seconds to say the same sentence in a measured test. Two habits narrow the choice quickly. First, press ▶ beside each candidate: every voice reads the same sample sentence, so the comparison is fair. Then generate the opening paragraph of your own text with the two or three you liked best; each result is labelled with its voice, so they are easy to play back to back.

Speed and pitch

Both controls are off until you tick their checkboxes, and both apply to the whole generation.

  • Speed (−100% to +100%) changes the tempo and therefore the length of the audio. In a test, −20% stretched a 56.6-second passage to 70.8 seconds. To hit an exact length, use the speed table in the AI voiceover guide.
  • Pitch (−100 to +100 Hz) moves the voice higher or lower without changing its timing: one sentence measured 6.05 seconds at −50 Hz, at 0 and at +50 Hz.

Small changes sound the most natural. A large pitch shift can make a voice sound processed, so for a clearly different character, choose another voice instead of pushing the pitch slider to its end.

Pauses and pacing

Commas and full stops already make the voice pause briefly. For an exact silence, type a marker such as [break:800ms] or [break:2s] where it should go, or press Ctrl+B (⌘+B on a Mac) to insert half a second at the cursor. Markers are not spoken and do not appear in the subtitles. One generation can hold 25 of them, none longer than 30 seconds and no more than 60 seconds in total.

Limitations

  • 5,000 characters per generation; longer writing is converted in parts with the same settings.
  • One voice per generation, so a conversation needs a separate generation for each speaker.
  • No custom or cloned voices and no emotion or speaking-style settings: speed, pitch and pauses are the only controls.
  • Your text is sent to Microsoft's online speech service to be voiced, so do not paste anything you may not share.
  • Results belong to the browser tab and are deleted about two hours after your last generation; download what you want to keep.
